\section{Counterfactual Commitment}\label{sec:counterfactual}
The observational results say that first errors and outcomes come apart, but not where a failing
trajectory actually becomes unrecoverable. We measure that directly by resampling.

\subsection{Setup}
\textbf{Generators and traces.} We use the three ProcessBench generators whose weights fit on two
16\,GB GPUs: Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct and Qwen2-1.5B-Instruct~\cite{qwen25}, all of whose traces we use
(\QwenSmallN\ and \QwenTwoN), and Qwen2.5-Math-7B-Instruct~\cite{qwen25math}, for which we use all
fatal and recovered traces with a recoverable gold answer and 60 clean traces (\QwenMathN\ in total;
3 traces without a gold answer are dropped). Gold answers come from the source datasets, matched on
problem text, and answers are compared with \texttt{math-verify}.

\textbf{Rollouts.} For every prefix $s_{1:k}$, $k=0,\dots,n-1$, we sample $m$ continuations from the
trace's own generator (\QwenSmallM, \QwenTwoM\ and \QwenMathM\ respectively; temperature 0.7, top-$p$
0.95; at most 1,536 new tokens for the 1.5B models and 1,024 for the 7B model) with vLLM~\cite{vllm}
in float16, and estimate $v_k$ as the fraction that reach the gold answer. In total we score
\TotalRollouts\ continuations from \QwenSmallPrefixes, \QwenTwoPrefixes\ and \QwenMathPrefixes\
prefixes. Every continuation, its answer and its correctness are released.

\textbf{What can and cannot be read off $v_k$.} Under the generating policy, $v_k$ is a martingale.
Curves conditioned on the eventual outcome therefore drift even when no single step matters: for
any fixed $k$ (and any stopping time),
\begin{equation}
\mathbb{E}[v_k \mid \text{fail}] = \frac{\mathbb{E}[v_k(1-v_k)]}{P(\text{fail})} \le v_0,
\label{eq:supermart}
\end{equation}
because $x(1-x)$ is concave, so $v_k(1-v_k)$ is a supermartingale. We therefore never interpret a
downward drift of failing curves by itself. We ask instead whether the drop is \emph{concentrated}
at the annotated error, against a position-matched null and the drop noise floor of clean traces,
and we evaluate backtracking policies with sample splitting.

\subsection{Where failing trajectories commit}
Table~\ref{tab:commit} classifies each fatal trace at $\delta=1/8$.

\textbf{Many failures are decided before the first step.} \QwenSmallCapPct\%, \QwenTwoCapPct\% and
\QwenMathCapPct\% of fatal traces are capability-limited: the model solves the problem from scratch
at most one time in eight, so no step of the trace is a choke point. This is the counterfactual
counterpart of Section~4's difficulty result, and it grows with the share of hard problems in a
model's traces. Shares at $\delta\in\{1/16,1/4\}$ move by a few points and do not change the ordering
(released tables).

\textbf{When the outcome is still open, the annotated error is often, but not usually exclusively,
where it closes.} Among fatal traces that are not capability-limited, the first error is the
largest drop in $v$ in \QwenSmallDec\ [\QwenSmallDecLo, \QwenSmallDecHi],
\QwenTwoDec\ [\QwenTwoDecLo, \QwenTwoDecHi] and \QwenMathDec\ [\QwenMathDecLo, \QwenMathDecHi] of
traces, against a position-matched null of \QwenSmallDecNull, \QwenTwoDecNull\ and
\QwenMathDecNull\ (the Qwen2-1.5B interval includes its null). Errors at the final step, where $v_n$ is the observed outcome, are rare
(\QwenMathDecLast\ for the 7B model) and excluding them leaves the shares unchanged
(\QwenSmallDecExcl, \QwenTwoDecExcl, \QwenMathDecExcl). The rest of these traces split between
\emph{latent commitment}, where success has already collapsed before the written error
(\QwenSmallLatentNL\%, \QwenTwoLatentNL\% and \QwenMathLatentNL\% of non-capability-limited fatal
traces), and \emph{recoverable errors}, after which success stays above $\delta$ until a later step
(\QwenSmallRecovNL\%, \QwenTwoRecovNL\% and \QwenMathRecovNL\%).

\textbf{Event study.} Aligned at the annotated error (Fig.~\ref{fig:event}), fatal traces show a step
change from $r=-1$ to $r=0$ (\QwenSmallEvFatBefore$\to$\QwenSmallEvFatAfter,
\QwenTwoEvFatBefore$\to$\QwenTwoEvFatAfter, \QwenMathEvFatBefore$\to$\QwenMathEvFatAfter), while
clean traces aligned at a random step drift smoothly upward, as the analogue of
Eq.~\ref{eq:supermart} for successes predicts. For recovered traces of the 7B model, success \emph{rises} across the annotated error
(\QwenMathEvRecBefore$\to$\QwenMathEvRecAfter), by far more than the success-conditioned drift of
clean traces (\QwenMathEvCleanBefore$\to$\QwenMathEvCleanAfter): for this model, a step that humans
mark as the first error often makes the correct answer more, not less, likely.

\subsection{Where to backtrack}
Verifier-guided search resamples a failing trace from some earlier point. We compare four targets on
fatal traces: restarting from scratch ($v_0$), resampling from just before the annotated first error
($v_{e-1}$), resampling from just before the decisive step ($v_{d-1}$), and the best prefix in
hindsight (an in-sample upper bound). The decisive step is an oracle quantity, but choosing it and
scoring it on the same continuations would reward noise. We therefore split the $m$ continuations of
every prefix at random into halves $A$ and $B$; half $A$ decides whether a trace is not
capability-limited ($v_0^A>\delta$) and where its decisive step is, and half $B$ scores every
policy. We average over 50 random splits and bootstrap over traces. On a planted-effect simulation
in which the first error is the decisive step by construction, this estimator returns a difference
of $+0.001$ between decisive-step and first-error backtracking, so it is not biased by the selection.

\textbf{Backtracking to the annotated first error is worse than starting over.} In all three models,
resampling from just before the human-annotated first error succeeds less often than restarting from
scratch, by \QwenSmallBtRestErr\ [\QwenSmallBtRestErrLo, \QwenSmallBtRestErrHi],
\QwenTwoBtRestErr\ [\QwenTwoBtRestErrLo, \QwenTwoBtRestErrHi] and \QwenMathBtRestErr\
[\QwenMathBtRestErrLo, \QwenMathBtRestErrHi] (Table~\ref{tab:backtrack}, Fig.~\ref{fig:backtrack}).
These are traces where the model can solve the problem, and the target is the expert label, i.e.\ a
perfect ProcessBench verifier. The correct steps before the error already lower the chance of
success: the prefix carries the failure before the error is written.

\textbf{Backtracking to where success drops recovers the loss at lower cost.} Resampling from just
before the decisive step beats first-error backtracking by \QwenSmallBtDiff, \QwenTwoBtDiff\ and
\QwenMathBtDiff\ (all CIs above zero; \QwenMathBtDiffAll\ for the 7B model with no selection at all)
and matches restarting (\QwenSmallBtDecRest, \QwenTwoBtDecRest, \QwenMathBtDecRest; all CIs include
zero) while generating \QwenSmallTokSave\%, \QwenTwoTokSave\% and \QwenMathTokSave\% fewer
continuation tokens. The best prefix in hindsight reaches \QwenSmallBtOracle, \QwenTwoBtOracle\ and
\QwenMathBtOracle, so there is headroom for a target chosen better than either.

\textbf{Reading.} Eq.~\ref{eq:supermart} explains why restart is a strong baseline: for any
backtracking point at a fixed depth, or at a stopping time of the trace, prefixes of failed
on-policy traces are on average no better than the empty prefix. The point just before the first
error is neither (it depends on the next step), so the inequality does not force first-error
backtracking below restart; the data show that it falls below anyway. A target helps only if it is
chosen with information about where success is lost. Correctness labels do not provide that information; value drops do. Verifier-guided
backtracking should therefore be evaluated against restart at matched compute, and verifiers meant
to steer search should be trained to find drops in $v$, not first errors.
